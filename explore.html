etymology

noun

Etymology is the study of words and where they came from.

Misunderstandings
create words

Misunderstandings can create words through a process called rebracketing. This happens when someone hears a phrase in another language but splits up the words in the wrong spots.

Rebracketing = putting the word breaks in a different place than the original speaker meant.

Case study

How a French phrase became “ammunition”

Follow the same sounds through three steps.

1French saysla munition“the military supplies”
→
2Heard asl’amunitionThe “a” seems to belong to the next word.
→
3English adoptsammunitionEnglish speakers reshape the form.
Same sounds, different split
What was meant
lamunition
What was heard
l’amunition
the “a” moved from the first word to the second

Why it happens: in fast speech there are no gaps between words, so listeners guess where one ends and the next begins.

Takeaway: the same sounds can be split into words in more than one way, and a wrong guess can become a new word.

Different languages can express the same meaning in completely different ways.

Each part adds another layer to the word’s meaning.

WHEN ONE WORD ISN’T ENOUGH

Mind the
translation gap.

Translation gaps occur when one language has a word, phrase, or grammatical feature that does not have an exact one-word equivalent in another language. The idea can still be translated—it may just take several words, an explanation, or a similar expression.

PORTUGUESE

saudade

No exact single-word translation in English.

“A deep feeling of missing someone or something, often mixed with sadness and longing.”

GERMAN

Schadenfreude

Pleasure from someone else’s misfortune.

ASL

Many signs in ASL represent a concept, not an English word, making direct translation tricky. One example is listing, which in English would be closer to a comma than a word.
