Teacher resource hub

How words are built,
borrowed, and misheard.

A flexible companion guide for grades 6–12 and introductory language or linguistics classes. Plan one 45–60 minute session, or split the ideas across two shorter lessons.

Open the Explore page ⟶Built for curious classrooms.

Start here

Give students a new way to see language.

This guide helps students explore four big ideas: misunderstandings can create new words, words are built from smaller meaningful parts, languages express the same meaning in different ways, and some ideas do not translate neatly from one language to another.

Students investigate examples from English, Spanish, French, German, Portuguese, Albanian, Haitian Creole, and American Sign Language (ASL), then practice with two mini-games.

Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain how rebracketing can turn a misheard phrase into a new word.
  • Define morphology and morphemes, then break a word into its morphemes.
  • Compare how different languages, including a signed language, express the same meaning.
  • Describe a translation gap and give an example.
  • Explain why an idiom’s literal meaning differs from its real meaning.

Materials

Gather these first.

  • Access to the Explore page on a projector or student devices
  • Whiteboard or chart paper
  • Sticky notes or index cards for optional hands-on activities
  • Optional: a short video of an ASL signer demonstrating the signs mentioned below

Key vocabulary

Words students will use.

Rebracketing

Hearing a phrase but dividing its sounds into word boundaries differently than the speaker intended.

Morphology

The study of how words are formed and how smaller parts called morphemes combine to create meaning.

Morpheme

The smallest part of a word that carries meaning, such as a root, prefix, or suffix.

Translation gap

When one language has a word, phrase, or grammar feature with no exact one-word equivalent in another language.

Idiom

A common phrase whose meaning is different from the literal meaning of its individual words.

Part 1 · Misunderstandings create words

Show how a boundary can move.

Teaching point. Misunderstandings can create words through rebracketing, or reanalysis. People hear a phrase but divide its sounds into word boundaries differently. If people repeat the new version often enough, it can become an accepted word.

Worked example. The French phrase la munition (“military supplies”) was heard and reanalyzed as l’amunition. English speakers altered this form into the word ammunition. Write la munition → l’amunition → ammunition on the board.

Mini-game: Word Boundary Detective. Students flip hidden cards to find matching pairs in the fewest moves or shortest time. Run it as a whole-class demo first, then let students play in pairs.

Part 2 · What is morphology?

Build meaning from smaller pieces.

Teaching point. Morphology is the study of how words are formed and how smaller parts of words, called morphemes, combine to create meaning.

Worked example. Unkindness = un- (not) + kind (caring or nice) + -ness (state or quality).

Mini-game: Morphology Brick Builder. Students choose the morpheme that fits the clue, add its brick to their tower, and reach the win line. Use cross-language examples and ask which morpheme carried the most meaning.

Part 3 · Morphology across languages

Same idea, different structures.

Different languages may express the same meaning through several small parts, one ending, or movement and space.

Englishun-help-ful-ness

not + help + full of + state

Spanishhabl-amos

speak + we; the ending shows the subject and verb tense

Albanianpa-dobishmëri-a

without/not + usefulness + the

Frenchin-util-ité

not + useful + state/quality

ASLHELP + NOT / NO-HELP

ASL uses signs, movement, space, facial expressions, and head movements rather than letters in a row.

Part 4 · Translation gaps

Hard to translate does not mean impossible.

Teaching point. A translation gap occurs when one language has a word, phrase, or grammatical feature without an exact one-word equivalent in another language. The idea can still be translated with several words, an explanation, or a similar expression.

saudadePortuguese

A deep feeling of missing someone or something, often mixed with sadness and longing.

SchadenfreudeGerman

Pleasure from someone else’s misfortune.

listingASL

A concept that would be closer to a comma than a word in English.

Part 5 · Fun facts — idioms

Literal pictures, real meanings.

Teaching point. An idiom is a common phrase whose meaning is different from the literal meaning of its individual words. Idioms show how different cultures picture the same idea.

Haitian CreoleM’ap boule

“I’m burning” → I’m doing good, I’m living.

FrenchIl pleut des cordes

“It is raining ropes” → It is raining heavily.

AlbanianTa ha zemrën

“I eat your heart” → I love you so much!

SpanishTirar la casa por la ventana

“Throw the house out the window” → To go all out.

Activity: Literal vs. Real. Read the literal meaning aloud and have students guess the real meaning before revealing it. Then ask students to share an idiom from a language they know.

In-class game suggestions

Morpheme Card Race

Write one morpheme on each index card and make a matching set for every team. Read a clue such as “the state of not being nice” or “we speak.” Teams race to lay out the morphemes in order. A correct build earns a point; explaining each piece earns a bonus.

Idiom Charades

Players act out or sketch the literal image of an idiom without speaking. Their team guesses the real meaning in one minute. A correct guess earns two points; a steal earns one.

Wrap-up · Exit ticket

Ask students to answer one:

  • Explain rebracketing and give the ammunition example.
  • Break one word into morphemes and say what each part means.
  • Name one thing that is hard to translate and explain why.
  • Share a favorite idiom and its real meaning.

Assessment ideas

Review exit tickets, note which morphemes students struggle with, or ask students to write about how one language builds or expresses meaning.

Teacher notes

Keep these ideas in view.

  • ASL is a full language with its own grammar, often expressed simultaneously through hands, face, head, and space.
  • “Untranslatable” usually means there is no tidy one-word match, not that the idea cannot be explained.
  • Words that began as mistakes can become real words when a community uses and understands them.
  • More word parts do not mean a more complex language; languages package meaning differently.
